### General Argument
_This will be revisted, as it would be bizarre to have never read Plato, and perfectly articulated this entire dialogue's structure and argument—and would warrant skepticism of intellectual content presented here if perfect—and because it imperfect, the commit history should reveal true intellectual groundwork._

The dialogue takes 3 parts: Socrates critically thinking through Cephalus, Polemarchus, and finally Thrasymachus. Cephalus represents the naive take on justice, Polemarchus represents the modern take of justice, and Thrasymachus represents the pessimistic realistic take on justice.

Cephalus never defines Justice, because that's not his aim or intent. He was asked about old age, and what wisdom he can share. Cephalus makes the claim that wealth offers the ability to pay off debts, and live out the end of life free from the burden of unpaid debts or having to lie to others, quoting Pindar "Sweet hope accompanies," and Socrates extracts the implied definition of Justice: paying off debts and telling the truth.

Polemarchus takes up the conversation after his father (i.e. Cephalus) with the sentiment that: "It is still my opinion that justice is helping friends and harming enemies." This results from the dialogue that turns up from a series of questions that reveal issues in this senitment, but Socrates' states in the steelman: "Justice, then, seems, according to you and Homer and Simonides, to be a certain art of stealing, for the benefit, to be sure, of friends and the harm of enemies."

Lastly, Thrasymachus offers a pessimistic realist position that views Justice as "The just is nothing other than the advantage of the stronger." Thrasymachus is taken most serious during Book I, and his argument lasts the longest.

Ultimately, the dialogue ends in aporia, which Book II starts and intends to settle.

### Meta-Themes

Bloom's introduction establishes the reason for this specific writing style: long, seemingly pedantic dialogue that works through questioning every step along the way. This style of writing is what Bloom asserts is Plato's style of teaching, to show the process of philosophizing through his writing, rather than writing down what he simply wanted to assert. And in this way, Book I shows the process of philosophical thinking rather than describing it.

Conversely, "How to Read a Book," by Mortimer J. Adler has an authorative structure where he commands readers do specific things (e.g., possibly, "Do not argue with the author until you have understood their argument.") Whereas, Plato's writing so far reflects the process of thinking itself, as a demonstration for students to understand how philosophizing functions.

The focus on Justice in Book 1's align with this Plato's goal of demonstrating the socratic method without explicitly spelling out the socratic method. Namely, Cephalus is given nearly no pushback when it becomes convenient to leave the discussion, whereas Thrasymachus tries to assert the answer to justice and leave, but his friends hold him to Socrates' questioning. This style of writing isn't simply decorative, but portrays how the socratic method is not equally distributed across all interlocutors.

Bloom postulates that Plato believes Socrates to have been a large proponent of philosophizing via intimate discussions with others, aiming to enlighten their thinking and forcing them to bring their assumptions to a head, rather than let others carry on without questioning. In Book I, Cephlalus represents someone who is near the end and doesn't have the will to be questioned, Polemarchus is all too eager to be questioned, and Thrasymachus represents those with a distain for such acts, viewing them as pedantic and the work of sophistry. And, this has aged well, becoming timeless as all three appear, in their different tempermens, today.

Lastly, Bloom asserts that Plat's long format dialogue is a direct approach to show students the process of thinking about philoposphy, not as a declarative work, but as a iterative and conversational type. For beginners, the argument seems pedantic and droll for it's soporific length. Additionally, it doesn't declare any resolve, quite literally ending with in aporia rather than arriving at a natural conclusion—inviting the rage of Thrasymachus perhaps.
