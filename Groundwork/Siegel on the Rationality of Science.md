# Introduction
Brian S. Baigrie critques Harvey Siegel (1985) in his "Siegel on the Rationality of Science." Essentially, Baigrie is pointing out that Siegel's project to place science in the a priori space using: "a general commitment to evidence." Baigrie is primarily concerned with the "unwelcome consequences" of this project; primarily that it's too permissive and doesn't cleanly individuate scientific belief from any other type of belief.

Baigrie takes Siegel's project, critiques it using the exact instances that Siegel dismisses, and explicitly drags the demarcation problem back over to empirical grounds. Siegel's claim is that the instrumental view of science offered by Laudan, is that scientific belief should be evaluated through rationality; as opposed to whether a method produces a good outcome.

At the surface, Siegel is correct. Simply dropping necessary a priori commitments in place of a dedication to good outcomes (e.g., "fruits of such inquiry") does seem malformed. During this time, philosophers of science talked about claims, hypotheses, and theories as mind-independent propositionally true entities. And so, the idea in the instrumentalist view was that these did not need to be evaluated rationally if they fit the instrumental analysis.

However, Baigrie is critical of Siegel's "provocative" thesis: instrumental science isn't sufficient, rationality is necessary. However, it isn't outright dismissal of Siegel's observation, it's a critique of Siegel's solution: "apriostic commitment to evidence." Baigrie writes,
> If Siegel is right in contending that an account of the rationality of science should address the rationality of scientific beliefs as well as the rationality of scientific inquiry, the question for Siegel is whether his aprioristic rendering of science's rationality allows us to draw a line between beliefs that are worth of acceptance and others that are not.

So, Baigrie agrees with that rationality is worth consideration, but questions Siegel's move for being too permissive. Baigrie writes, "Does it furnish a line between genuine scientific beliefs and psuedoscientific ones?" This is the crux of the whole paper, and the major theme Baigrie will hammer home. And so, Baigrie sets out to steelman the position and then pressure test it.

One of the distinctions Baigrie makes pertains to the Scientific Method (SM), which he states Siegel rejects as being methodological, and rather, epistemic. Serving to evaluate the justification of scientific subject matter like theories, rather than being mistaken as a singular method that pretends science doesn't require methodological plurality.

The main point that Siegel is making, is that the SM is not a statically bound generalization for science. Instead, he's correctly arguing that if a SM exists, then it's necessary that it consists of general characterizations that are informed contigent on the period science exists in. The example he provides demonstrates this cleanly: the general principles are [explanatory adequacy] + [insistence on testing] + [commitment to inductive support] but general principle of the SM depends on the scientific period in question (however adequacy is conceived) + (however testing is to be best done) + (however inductive inference is through to be best made).

Siegel is correctly identifying that any postualated SM must contain timeless general principles like those bracketed, of which, serve as placeholders to survive any "periods of scientific upheaval," by remaining contigently dependent on the time period in question, with those in parentheses.

However, Baigrie is pushing back on the single overreach Siegel makes when he attempts to ground the SM in "a commitment to evidence." The reason Siegel is making this move is because, despite his ingenuity to see that the SM would require a specific epistemic structure to survive "scientific upheaval", it still lacks a shared criterion that makes it necessary at all. And so, rather than allowing it to be purely rational, without a necessary condition; it remains instrumental, and falls into the same category he's attempting to escape. So, Siegel grounds that "specific methodological criteria of science," in _"a commitment to evidence."_

This is the specific point that Baigrie chews on. He quotes Siegel:
> ... so as to establish the evidentiary support of hypotheses and theories. The hallmark of SM is its commitment to the establishment of the epistemic worth of scientific claims by virtue of the establishment of the evidential support of those claims.

Immediately after designating the need for a commitment to evidentiary support, Baigrie correctly puts the questions directly on what makes this commitment _"scientific"_ to begin with. It's the classical move of undermining the position by asking whether it's sawing off the branch it sits on. In my mind, it's a clean move when it works, but a lazy one. However, Baigrie doesn't end his critique here, he begs the question as to whether this commitment is even necessarily science-specific, saying, "Science is surely not the only discipline that establishes the worthiness of hypotheses on the basis of evidence."

### An Aside
I'm disconnecting this new thought from the analysis of the essay, because I'm going to invoke the presentism fallacy to critique a sub-point in this essay. Immediately, after this quote, he lists 3 examples: "engineering, the courts, the social sciences," which highlights a bidirectional historical pull between science expanding it's boundaries (encorachment) and also fields that were not previously considered scientific attempting to get technical to purchase membership and gain the credibility of belonging to science.

This framing has a lot of issues, and at a surface level, it's definitely inaccurate. However, the deeper sentiment I wanted to point out was what is being implied here: that scientism in the last half-century has encroached on fields, but fields also wanted the _science_ social credibility token.

---

Baigrie even quotes Siegel making this point directly, "For I am not claiming that there is anything about SM which is unique to science in the sense that only science does or can do that unique thing." However, Siegel is making a specific point that individuates this the SM from other disciplines, he says, "science typically, or at least ideally, manifests and organizes its affairs with specific attention to the commitment to evidence." Siegel is not wrong, he's individuating that science does orient itself toward a commitment to evidence, an observation about science that has aged well. Baigrie doesn't necessarily disagree either, he restates Siegel's conclusion cleanly: while others _may_, science universally _does_.

Before getting into the next point, it's worth recalling that Baigrie does not disagree with Siegel's project: ground science aprioristically to discredit science as purely instrumental analysis. Baigrie is arguing something more specific: that Siegel is too permissive with the necessary a priori condition of the SM being: _a comittment to evidence._

And so, Baigrie quotes Siegel conceding that he recognizes not all intellectual pursuits require their beliefs and justifications to be evidentiary. Mainly, as Baigrie states, Siegel is pointing to psuedosciences and folk belief systems. And more critically, he shows that Siegel charges these with inerrancy and insularity when he says "are held in spite of, or systematically protected from, contrary evidence." And the charge that Baigrie delivers is: are you truly drawing the line between science and psuedoscience on the basis of evidentiary norms? As if, to point out, how weak a point this would be to settle the demarcation problem.

Baigrie brings up a contradiction in Siegel's project. He quotes Siegel making the claim that rationality tracks evidence, but earlier, Siegel said psuedoscience lacks any evidentiary norm. So the issue is that, Siegel already conceded and stated that evidence is not uniquely bounded to science and can appear outside science. So, as the principled basis for demarcating between science and non-science, a commitment to evidence doesn't hold if it both excluded psuedoscience and includes non-science.

That's pretty technical though, so let's take a step back and look at what Siegel is attempting to do with this essay. Essentially, Siegel recognizes something that has aged well since he wrote his essay: Science has a unique relationship with evidentialism; other domains may have a relationship with evidentialism, or torment their relationship with evidence, but none is as unique as science's relationship.

This isn't a point for scientism either. It's true of science, it has specific ways to evaluate and standardize our upstream commitments to what good evidence looks like, and how different fields of science justify the knowledge they produce. In essence, Siegel is intuitively capturing the spirit of science, but he's not asserting it without arguing for it. Now, Baigrie is specifically looking to exploit that gap in Siegel's writing: an assertion is not an argument.

When Siegel makes the positive assertion, "Science has a commitment to evidence, and many fields do as well, but psuedoscience excludes evidence," he does not provide a principled basis for why psuedoscience is excluded, but more importantly, he never provides a specific in what way science has a unique relationship to evidence... Only that is does.

Baigrie pushes this point to its natural conclusion, stating,

> It is clearly not the nature of its specific claims; there is
 nothing intrinsically unscientific about the belief concerning the invari-
 ability of species, a point that Siegel no doubt would be willing to con-
 cede.

And then proposes his diagnosis of these sorts of claims. Mainly, that Creation Science claims operate under a connection to evidence that is _methodologically suspect_ and designed to masquerade as scientific claims. Moreoso, he's arguing that psuedoscientific methods are based on fake norms that take the shape of real ones. He's using scientific methodological conceptions to compare Creation Science methods against, and it becomes apparent they're visibily rigorous, but their actual structure is misconstrued and malformed.

Baigrie imports Karl Popper's critique of Marxism as supporting evidence for his diagnosis, stating that while it has an abundance of empirical support (i.e., evidence per verificationism testability), it does not adhere well to current (well, at the time of this writing) norms and epistemic practices that inform testability in science. In simpler words, Marxism is only empirically verified, which is a simpler testability framework, whereas it remains untestable by updated conceptions of testability in science. Baigrie is likely nodding toward the falsification versus older verificationism argument here, as he then states that Philip Kitcher makes the same critique of Creation Science: "draws its epistemological support from certain unacceptable methodological views."

> The real objection to such creationist claims as that of the (relative)
 invariability of species is not that such invariability has not been ex-
 plained by scientific laws, but rather that the evidence for invaria-
 bility is less robust than the evidence for its contrary, variability. But
 to say as much requires renunciation of [the view] . . . that Crea-
 tionism is not testable. (Laudan)

When discussing what Creation Sciences does, Baigrie's focus remains on methodological grounds. He makes the distinction that Creation Science isn't insensitive to methodological requirements, but their _specific construal_ of those requirements. Which is mainly a critique against Creation Sciences aprioristic requirements, which does align with Siegel's focus on aprioristic requirements as well. Creation Science intentionally distorts their relationship with evidence so their final conclusions are protected, whereas, as Siegel might defend something like, Science continually updates their relationship with evidence so no final conclusions are supported at all, but instead, constantly revising the grounds of justification any scientific claim sits on. Though, I think that's likely a bit too nebulous and strict.

Siegel is taken to postulate that contrary to Baigrie's diagnosis, Creation Science is simply suspending their relationship with evidential support which as Baigrie is pointing out, does not hold. Baigrie takes this a bit too far though, and says, "After all, who among us is not "committed" to evidence," which might be targetting a stricter sense of evidentialism that Siegel doesn't make. In fact, it might misrepresenting Siegel's view entirely, who postulated earlier that not all human intellectual pursuits or projects require their beliefs and justification to be evidentiary. It would seem, Siegel and Baigrie have a mismatched calibration of evidentialism, or more specifically, what constitutes proper evidence.

This notion of mismatched calibration to evidentialism isn't a problem of the past either. This still appears in epistemology, especially regarding what counts as evidence. For example, the Bayesian framing is argubly more permissive than Siegel's conception, which is that evidence is whatever updates how likely you are to believe something. This fits perfectly into all sorts of non-science frameworks. So, part of this debate seems to be the difference in how strict/permissive each thinker is taking evidence to mean, and likely, that Baigrie views Siegel's as too permissive.

The next point Baigrie makes is the final point in dissecting Siegel, before introducing the unwelcome consequences portion of his essay. Baigrie argues that Siegel's view of evidentiary commitment is so permissive, that any other field could claim they too contain a scientific method of inquiry, as long as nobody postulates the SM is an algorithimic procedure for discovery. Put more plainly, the SM depends on a weakened comittment to evidence, and if that's the case, anyone could formulate the SM in their own view of evidence. But more importantly, the implication that anyone can support any belief under Siegel and call it scientific, so long as it abides by even a tinge of evidentialism.

This, in fact, is a killing blow because it lowers the threshold so low that anything can cross over and claim to be scientific: including Creation Science, or Marxist Historical Materialism.

And so, Baigrie brings the smuggled polysemy of "commitment" to a head, and charges Siegel for not critically analyzing it. Which, ironically, is likely what Siegel had in mind but took it in a different direction.
