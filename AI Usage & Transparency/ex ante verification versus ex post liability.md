# Ex Ante Verification versus Ex Post Liability

When it comes to intellectual authorship, the line is blurred with AI. But, this isn't a new phenomena. For instance, PhilPaper has always been filled with a plethora of intellectual work that rides a fine line between absolute genius or total tomfoolery. And, there's no real criteria to discern which is which. Instead, the intellectual work itself must face scrutiny, and survive. This model of making claims in the public space is what I call Open Epistemic Territory. Anyone, anywhere, at anytime, past or future, can make a claim that, once made, is subject to the criticism, scrutiny, and judgement of all other claim makers in the same space.

Hypatia, Will of Ockham, Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, Mary Wollstonecraft, Jane Addams, John Rawls, Slavoj Zižek... It doesn't matter, each of them has written, contributed, and shared their intellectual thoughts in open epistemic waters. And, as time as gone on, their thoughts have been tested, pushed, stressed, and for many, fallen apart under conceptual analysis, technology, and better evidence. However, they still contribute something unique that outlives any of their individual propositional claims and has engraved them into their respective history books: their ability to think critically and take ownership over their intellectual contributions for better or worse.

## Open Epistemic Territory (OET)

I often replace "territory" with other words like "waters" or "landscapes" depending on context, and the point I'm trying to make. OET is akin to a open PVP arena, or play area in a video game. Anyone can enter and propose their ideas, but they're now open to the same degree of scrutiny as anyone else who also makes claims. For instance, someone from the Church of Scientology can make an empirical claim, but it doesn't necessarily mean their claim will survive in OET. Someone with a strong philosophy of science background will make quick work of any conceptual claims regarding eternal contracts and Zenu.

Eventually, this allows for the selection of better thinkers (i.e., versatile, open-minded individuals with substantiated ideas) to continue to thrive against the scrutiny of others; whereas, insular thinkers tend to find communities that insulate themselves and ideas from the critiques of others to preserve their ideas, rather than update based off new findings, evidence, or conceptual analysis. Even this idea of OET, itself, would fall into the OET as others would stress test it, and remain skeptical of what it attempts to license.

In OET, there is not protected status or privilege. Whereas, in closed epistemic spaces, protected status is entirely permissible.

Recently, Bishop Kane wrote about how Christians should occupy the highest seats of power in government, and lead research in multiple fields of science including Genetics. He's influential in his community, and respected as a serious theologian and contributor contemporary theological literature. He writes a book title _The Mandate of Influence: Reclaiming the Cultural Mountain._ He argues in it that economic and political influence is a modern form of biblical stewardship, and the field of genetics specifically needs more Christian influence.

Bishop Kane flew out to the American Society of Human Genetics Annual Meeting, which hosted over 7,000 attendees from more than 75 countries. He spoke to a few hundred of them: PIs, clinical geneticists, genetic counselors, data scientists, reagent developers, and their trainees, including postdoctoral fellows and graduate students. During which, the audience members quickly and aggressively challenged Bishop Kane on statistics, peer-reviewed citations, and the empirical evidence he offers.
