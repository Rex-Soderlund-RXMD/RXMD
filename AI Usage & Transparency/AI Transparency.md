# AI Transparency

**AI-usage is controversial.** Mainly because it masks what counts as original thought. Critics are rightfully critical of AI-usage when traces of AI-output appear in writers who do not disclose AI-usage in the production of their intellectual property. The dubious nature of what counts as original thought is becoming blurry as AI-usage increases, and disclosure continues to remain a serious contention in ethics writing.

For many, the debate centered around AI-usage in writing is black-or-white: to use AI, or to dispel AI-use.

RXMD AI-usage isn't as straight-forward as: yes, I use AI, or no, I don't use AI. And, if anyone's disclosure is that simple, they're missing techinical nuance this paper explores about just how deep AI-usage goes, and why the line is becoming nearly continous.

#### The Alibi Paper

There is a unique paper that specifically puts the entire process of AI-usage on display, as part of the paper's content (i.e., AI is prompted to write a paper on AI-transparency while documenting the entire process through a publically-available iterative process) and then the final claim the author of the paper makes is: **a reader can use the entire record of the AI producing the paper to deny that the author wrote the paper.** Which, is a mind-bending concept to wrap your head around.

The paper is an attempt to build an exact system for academic ethics writing using AI-assisted writing. Michele Loi, PhD from University of Milan, publishes a paper called _[The Journal of Prompt Engineered (Moral) Philosophy](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.08639)_, and it seriously blurs the line between whether AI-generated content is worth the merit or not.

Essentially, the author prompted the AI to write and document the entire process until the paper was ready to publish under his PhD credentials—without him having read a single word of the output; and he does this while handing over the very fact that any reader could **give him his own paper as proof he didn't write the paper.** The paper is entirely AI-generated, but the author stands behind the merit of the paper's philosophical contribution. The author is underspecific as to whether the paper is a stunt, or an actual contribution to the field.

However, the paper explicitly writes out its AI-usage, and interestingly writes:

> Source conversations. The raw transcripts from which the above artifacts are derived are not part of the archive. They are retained by the author, indexed by session identifier (SID-YYYYMMDDHHMMSS for Stage III and CFP; chat UUIDs for v1/v2), and available upon request.

Instead of allowing raw transcripts to serve as real-time artifacts of a work in progress, Michele Loi treats derived artifacts (guidance docs, reivison logs) as the actual archive. Meaning, the public-facing record is synthesized and selectively curated after the work was done, rather than in real-time. So, regardless of the Author's hand in the curation of the content, and whether the paper contains author-backed merit or is an elaborate meta-stunt, _Michele does not release the AI transcripts used to generate the paper._

### Against Documentation and a Critique of Trajectory

One of the issues with being transparent about AI-usage is the degree to which AI-usage is disclosed. Initially, it may seem as simple as making explicit: "Co-written with AI." However, what's more difficult to make explicit isn't what is documented, but to what degree the trajectory of a writer's thinking was influenced by AI-use.

For instance, simply providing a Claude.ai/share public link may seem radically transparent, but what's more difficult to ascertain is how AI shaped what a writer's thoughts and thinking process were influenced by the conversation with AI. Analogously, a principle investigator will shape how a lab's researchers will think, which skews how much intellectual independence the researchers are really assigned. In practice, PI's are cited first on a paper which constitutes intellectual property of that lab (whether as a historical artifact, or an act of epistemic integrity is contentious).

This doesn't translate as well epistemically for writers. Does Claude.ai appear first in an article, or as a co-author, or only cited as a tool-of-use? Many writers would be hesitant to include Claude.ai as a co-collaborator because the line between how much an AI contributes is argubly contested; not just in propositional-value, but assumed intellectual independence and trajectory.

#### Against Documentation

Regarding citing AI as a tool-of-use, rather than a co-collaborator, [Geraldo Xexéo at UFRJ](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25346v1#:~:text=can%20be%20added.-,4,Extended%20Facets,-The%20extended%20model) details the intent of AI-use by asking why AI was used. He details a scale:
1. Intent 1 (I1) is spelling and grammar.
2. I2 is translating or rewriting existing text.
3. I3 is producing new prose from scratch.
4. I4 is conceptual work, proposing categories, structuring an argument, spotting a gap.

I4 smuggles in the conceptual work that critics of AI-usage attack, though, it's not limited to I4 and could appear in I1. However, each of these is documentable in practice. For instance, having AI explain complex philosophical work into simpler langauge could be cited explcitly.

Github is uniquely positioned to provide an entire commit history, which functions to allow real-time documentation of AI-use as the writing unfolds. Which provides a historical accrediation of the intellectual process, by explicitly documenting with Claude.ai/share URL links to specific intellectual work (i.e., sentences, paragraphs, etc).
